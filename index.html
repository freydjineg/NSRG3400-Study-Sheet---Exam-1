🔬 Module 1: Mental Health, Mental Illness & Theories

Understanding the continuum of mental health, DSM-5, stigma, and the theoretical frameworks that guide psychiatric nursing.

💡

The BIG idea: Mental health and illness exist on a continuum. Stigma, culture, and biology all influence how we define, diagnose, and treat psychiatric disorders.

🌡️ The Mental Health Continuum

Mental health and illness are not binary — they exist on a dynamic spectrum. The same person may function at different levels week to week.

🔑 ResiliencyThe ability to recover from or adjust successfully to trauma or change. Most people have it and it can be developed. Resilient people recognize feelings, deal with them, and learn — they don't avoid them.

📋 DSM-5 & Stigma

DSM-5 Classifies Disorders, Not PeopleProvides a common language for clinicians, researchers, and policymakers. A mental disorder = clinically significant syndrome associated with distress, disability, or increased risk. Use person-first language: "person WITH schizophrenia."
Cultural Formulation Interview (CFI)DSM-5 acknowledges Western bias. The CFI and glossary of cultural concepts of distress (e.g., ataque de nervios, susto) reduce cultural misdiagnosis.
🚨 Stigma — #1 Barrier to TreatmentNegative stereotype → fear, rejection, social isolation, lost employment, healthcare disparities. Over 60% of those with mental illness receive NO treatment in 12 months. Average delay to first treatment: 6–23 years.
Historical Stigma ExamplesHomosexuality in DSM until 1973. Women who worked outside the home once considered mentally ill. These show how cultural/political norms shape what is called "mental illness."

📊 Epidemiology

Prevalence1 in 5 adults per year. ~50% lifetime. Many have co-occurring (dual) diagnoses.
Treatment GapOver 60% receive no treatment in 12 months. Patients more likely to see PCP or spiritual advisor than psychiatry first.
High-Burden DisordersMDD: leading cause of disability in US; 2× more women. Anxiety: 18.1% of population. Schizophrenia: 1.1%.
Mental Health Parity Act (1996) & ACA (2010)Requires equal insurance coverage for mental health. ACA banned annual dollar limits and pre-existing condition exclusions. Depression screening covered preventatively.
Disorders With Strong Biological InfluencesSchizophrenia, bipolar disorder, major depression, OCD, panic disorder, PTSD, autism, anorexia nervosa, ADHD. These can be seen as diseases with an underlying biological component — but biological factors alone do not explain full severity and course.

🔍 Freud Deep Dive + Behavioral Therapy Types

Freud — Levels of Awareness

  • Conscious: What we are aware of right now
  • Preconscious: Not immediately in awareness but retrievable (memories, stored knowledge)
  • Unconscious: Repressed thoughts, feelings, memories — NOT easily accessible; contains the id; drives behavior without our knowledge
Psychoanalytic Therapy — Practical FactsGoal: uncover unconscious truths. Process: free association, dream analysis, analysis of transference. Duration: 3-5 times/week for many years. Time-consuming, expensive, and emotionally painful. Now largely replaced by shorter-term evidence-based approaches.

Behavioral Therapy Types

Systematic DesensitizationClassical conditioning based. Learns: gradually face the feared stimulus while relaxed. Used for phobias, PTSD avoidance. Example: agoraphobia treated by first visualizing outdoor trips while relaxing.
Aversion TherapyClassical + operant conditioning. Associates unpleasant consequences with unwanted behavior. Example: disulfiram (Antabuse) — taking alcohol while on Antabuse = severe nausea/vomiting/dizziness. Also used with sex offenders.
BiofeedbackTechnique where individuals learn to control physiological responses (heart rate, BP, breathing, brain waves, skin temperature) through real-time visual or auditory feedback, then using relaxation techniques. Now available through smartphone apps and wearables.
Watson's "Little Albert" ExperimentJohn B. Watson conditioned a 9-month-old infant to fear white fur/hair. He concluded anyone could be trained to be anything through behavioral techniques. Note: now considered ethically unacceptable. Demonstrated that fear responses can be conditioned through classical conditioning.

TheoristTheoryKey Contribution

Erik EriksonPsychosocial Development8 stages across the lifespan, each with a developmental crisis (trust vs. mistrust, autonomy vs. shame, etc.). Failure to resolve = personality/psychiatric issues.

Maslow's Full Hierarchy (Bottom to Top)Physiological (food, water, sleep) → Safety (security, stability) → Love and Belonging (relationships, community) → Esteem (confidence, achievement, respect) → Self-Actualization (reaching full potential) → Self-Transcendence (service to others, spiritual connection beyond the self — sometimes shown as 7th level). Lower needs must be met before higher needs emerge.

🏛️ Peplau's Anxiety Levels & Group/Family Therapy

1
Mild: Day-to-day alertness. Full perception. Learning occurs easily. Normal baseline.
2
Moderate: Heightened awareness but narrowed perceptual field. Learning possible with direction.
3
Severe: Greatly diminished perception. Nearly all behavior directed at reducing anxiety. Cannot think clearly.
4
Panic: Overwhelming. Paralysis OR dangerous hyperactivity. Cannot communicate, function, or follow directions.
Yalom's 11 Curative Factors of Group TherapyAltruism, cohesiveness, interpersonal learning, guidance, catharsis, identification, family reenactment, self-understanding, instillation of hope, universality, existential factors.
Tuckman's Group Stages & Recovery ModelForming → Storming → Norming → Performing → Adjourning. Recovery model: NOT about cure — about living adaptively WITH chronic illness. Emphasizes hope, empowerment, meaning, and patient as partner.

⚖️ Module 2: Settings, Legal & Ethical Practice + Anger/Violence

Where psychiatric care happens, how patients are admitted, and the legal/ethical framework every nurse must know cold.

💡

The BIG idea: Psychiatric patients have the same civil rights as everyone else. The nurse must balance individual rights against public safety — and document everything.

📅 History of Psychiatric Care Settings

1
Pre-Civil War: Asylums created for the "insane." Danvers State Hospital is a historical example of early institutionalization.
2
1940s-1950s: Advent of psychotropic drugs (chlorpromazine/Thorazine, 1950s — first antipsychotic). Only two options: private psychiatrist's office or state mental hospital.
3
1960s — Great Society Reform: Medicare and Medicaid created. State-run psychiatric hospital population declined. Community Mental Health Centers Act (Kennedy, 1963) — preference for community care.
4
1999 — Olmstead Decision: Supreme Court ruled institutionalization is "unjustified isolation" violating ADA. Community-care systems largely replaced state hospitals.

Financing Psychiatric Care

SSDI vs SSISSDI (Social Security Disability Insurance) = for people who have worked and paid Social Security taxes; becomes disabled. SSI (Supplemental Security Income) = for people with extreme economic need regardless of work history. 6 in 10 SSDI recipients under 65 have a psychiatric diagnosis.
NAMINational Alliance on Mental Illness — the largest nonprofit grassroots mental health organization in the US. Encourages self-help and promotes recovery. Grassroots groups confront stigma, influence policies, and support the rights of those who are mentally ill.
1996 Wellstone-Domenici Mental Health Parity ActLarge group health plans cannot impose annual or lifetime dollar limits on mental health benefits less favorable than limits on medical/surgical benefits. Strengthened by the ACA.

Specialty Treatment Settings

  • Pediatric psychiatric care: Child/adolescent inpatient and outpatient units
  • Geriatric psychiatric care: Specialized units for older adults (dementia, late-life depression)
  • Forensic psychiatric care: Court-ordered evaluations; competency to stand trial; not guilty by reason of insanity
  • Veterans Administration (VA) centers: PTSD, TBI, substance use, suicide prevention programs
  • Alcohol and drug abuse treatment: Detox, residential, outpatient; integrated dual-diagnosis programs

Multidisciplinary Treatment Team

  • Psychiatrist: Diagnosis, medication management, overall treatment plan
  • PMH-APRN: Prescriptive authority, psychotherapy, consultation
  • Psychologist: Psychological testing, psychotherapy
  • Social Worker: Discharge planning, community resources, family therapy
  • Occupational Therapist (OT): ADL skills, cognitive rehabilitation
  • Recreational Therapist: Therapeutic activities, milieu groups
  • Mental Health Technician/Aide: Direct patient care, safety monitoring
  • Physician Assistant (PA): Medical management under physician supervision

🚪 Admission Procedures & Legal Status

Voluntary AdmissionPatient seeks admission through written application. Has the right to demand and obtain release at any time. Most states allow a temporary hold for re-evaluation before discharge. May be converted to involuntary status.
Involuntary Admission — Three Criteria(1) Dangerous to self or others, (2) Unable to meet basic needs due to mental illness, (3) Legally detained and poses escape risk. Patient retains: freedom from unreasonable restraint, right to informed consent, right to refuse medications.

⚕️ Patient Rights & Informed Consent

Right to Treatment (1964)Humane environment + qualified/sufficient staff + individualized plan. Cannot confine a non-dangerous person who can survive safely in the community.
Informed Consent Requires:Nature of condition; purpose/nature of treatment; risks AND benefits; alternative options; probability of success; risks of NOT consenting. Presence of psychosis ≠ incompetent — incompetency is determined by courts.
🚨 Restraint & Seclusion — CMS RulesONLY when immediate physical safety is at risk. Duration limits: Adults 18+ → 4 hrs; Children 9-17 → 2 hrs; Under 9 → 1 hr. MD/LIP must evaluate face-to-face within 1 HOUR. Monitor every 15-30 min. 150 people die annually — NO therapeutic value.

🔒 Confidentiality, HIPAA & Tarasoff

HIPAA (Effective 2003)Cannot disclose patient information without consent to ANYONE — including family. Psychotherapy notes kept separate from general records. Privacy continues AFTER DEATH. Discussions in public places even without patient name = violation.
🚨 Tarasoff — Duty to Warn"The protective privilege ends where public peril begins." When a patient presents serious danger to an identified person: (1) assess danger, (2) identify specific threatened individual, (3) notify victim/family/police. Now law in most states.

⚖️ Key Legal Concepts & Court Cases

Concept/CaseYearWhat It Established
Writ of Habeas CorpusCommon LawFormal written court order requiring a custodian to bring a detained person before the court to determine if detention is LAWFUL. A patient's right to challenge involuntary confinement.
Humphrey v. Cady1972Involuntary civil commitment to a mental hospital is a "massive curtailment of liberty" requiring due process protections. Governments must follow fair procedures before depriving someone of liberty.
Canterbury v. Spence1972Right to self-determination. An adult with sound mind has the right to determine what shall be done with his or her own body. Basis for informed consent law.
Olmstead v. L.C.1999Institutionalization is "unjustified isolation" violating ADA. Least restrictive community placement required when appropriate.
Tarasoff v. Regents of UC1974/1976Duty to warn identified third parties of credible threats. Therapist liability for failure to protect.
Types of Hospital Discharge
  • Conditional Release: Patient is discharged but must comply with conditions (e.g., outpatient treatment, medication compliance, no substance use). Can be returned to hospital if conditions violated.
  • Unconditional Release (Discharge): Full discharge with no ongoing legal strings attached. Patient has full autonomy.
Purposes of Medical Records
  1. Provide accurate and complete information about the CARE and TREATMENT of patients
  2. Give health care personnel a means of COMMUNICATING with each other
  3. Allow for CONTINUITY OF CARE
Medical records are also LEGAL DOCUMENTS — chart records data made in the normal course of care. Nurses should document accurately, descriptively, and legibly.
Dependent AdultsProtected by law from physical or fiduciary neglect or abandonment. In most states, willfully failing to report suspected abuse, neglect, or exploitation of a disabled adult is a misdemeanor. Laws require reporting knowledge and reasonable suspicion of mental abuse or suffering.
Least Restrictive Alternative DoctrineMandates that the LEAST DRASTIC MEANS be taken to achieve a specific purpose. A patient cannot be hospitalized if they can be adequately treated in the community. This applies at every level: for agitation, try verbal intervention before medications; try medications before restraints; try restraints before seclusion.
Exceptions to Confidentiality: Duty to warn identified victims (Tarasoff) | Child/elder abuse reporting (all 50 states) | HIV/STD partner notification (state-specific)

😡 Anger, Aggression & Violence (Ch. 24)

Risk Factors for Violence

  • #1 predictor: History of previous violence
  • Childhood aggression = strongest predictor of adult violence
  • Alcohol/substance intoxication or withdrawal
  • Command hallucinations (psychosis)
  • Neurobiological: limbic system, amygdala, low serotonin
  • Settings most at risk: psychiatric units, EDs, waiting rooms

Pre-Assaultive Warning Signs

  • Hyperactivity (pacing, slamming) = most critical
  • Angry/irritable affect; increasing tension
  • Verbal abuse; loud voice; clenched jaw/fist
  • Intense or avoidant eye contact; suspiciousness
De-escalation Principles
  • Appear calm; speak softly and slowly
  • Stand at 45° angle — not confrontational
  • Sit at patient level; do NOT tower
  • Do not speak while patient is yelling
  • Set clear, enforceable limits
  • "What will help you right now?"
  • Never turn your back on an angry patient
  • Have sufficient staff visible ("show of strength")
Comfort Room — Recovery Model AlternativeVoluntary self-management space. Trauma-informed approach: safety, trustworthiness, peer support, collaboration, empowerment, cultural sensitivity. Evidence shows reduction in seclusion/restraint use.

😤 Violence — Precise Definitions & Deep Detail

Precise Definitions

AngerA normal, universal emotional response to frustration, threat, or challenge. NOT inherently pathological. Varies from mild irritation to intense fury and rage. No judgment should be passed on it.
AggressionAnger's motor counterpart — goal-directed action resulting in verbal or physical attack. Can be appropriate (self-protective or protecting self from bullying). NOT the same as violence.
ViolenceThe unjust, unwarranted, or unlawful display of verbal threats, intimidation, or physical force with INTENT to cause property damage, personal injury, or death. Always has the discrete intention of doing harm.

Comorbidities Associated With Violence

  • PTSD — hyperarousal, flashbacks, impulsivity
  • Substance abuse — strongest environmental clinical factor (intoxication or withdrawal)
  • Depression — particularly with psychomotor agitation
  • Anxiety disorders — panic, paranoia
  • Psychosis — command hallucinations especially dangerous
  • Personality disorders — antisocial and borderline
🚨 Childhood Red Flags for Adult Violence
  • Setting fires in childhood
  • Animal cruelty
  • Diagnosis of conduct disorder
The classic triad — along with family history of violence. These are red flags for future violent behavior.

Milieu Characteristics That INCREASE Violence Risk

Environmental Risk Factors in Clinical Settings
  • Loud, chaotic environment
  • Overcrowding — inadequate personal space
  • Staff inexperience
  • Provocative or controlling staff behavior
  • Poor limit setting by staff
  • Staff inconsistency (arbitrary revocation of privileges)
These are all modifiable — good milieu management PREVENTS incidents.
Post-Assaultive Stage: Critical Incident DebriefingAfter every violent incident, conduct staff analysis and critical incident debriefing. Purpose: assures quality of care, allows staff to critically examine their response, and identifies improvement opportunities. Staff self-care is also essential — vicarious trauma is real.
Pharmacologic Management of AggressionAcute: benzodiazepines (lorazepam/Ativan first-line IM) and second-generation antipsychotics for emergencies. Chronic: anticonvulsants, antipsychotics, beta-blockers, buspirone, clonidine, lithium, SSRIs — for ongoing management of chronic aggressive behavior patterns.
Monitoring During Restraints/SeclusionMonitor every 15 minutes face-to-face for needs, safety, and comfort. Persons under age 14 require CONSTANT face-to-face observation (not just through a window). Gradual reintegration: move from 4-point to 2-point restraints, then planned time-outs from seclusion that are gradually lengthened.

💬 Module 3: Communication & Therapeutic Relationships

The nurse–patient relationship IS the intervention in psychiatric nursing. Communication is a clinical skill.

💡

The BIG idea: 90% of communication is nonverbal. Therapeutic techniques require practice. Transference and countertransference must be recognized and managed.

📡 The Communication Process

1
Stimulus — Need to communicate (information, comfort, advice)
2
Sender — Initiates contact; message filtered through biases, culture, mood, experience
3
Message — Expressed verbally OR nonverbally (visual, tactile, auditory, silence)
4
Receiver — Interprets message through THEIR own filters
5
Feedback — Response to validate accuracy. Every message passes through TWO sets of filters.
The 90% RuleOnly 5-10% of communication is verbal. 65-95% is nonverbal: body posture, facial expressions, eye contact, touch, tone/pitch/rate of voice, appearance, silence, sighing, fidgeting.
Congruent vs. Incongruent MessagesCongruent: verbal and nonverbal match. Double message: they conflict. "I'm fine" while crying = nonverbal tells the truth. Always trust the nonverbal.
🚨 Miscommunication = 3rd Leading Cause of Medical Death210,000–440,000 preventable deaths per year. Improving accuracy can reduce medical errors by 30%.

🌍 Cross-Cultural Communication

4 Most Problematic Areas

  • Communication styles: Some cultures are expressive; others value emotional restraint — a quiet smile can mean many things
  • Eye contact: Hispanic, Asian, Native American cultures may avoid eye contact with authority as RESPECT (not disinterest)
  • Touch: Some cultures welcome it; others prefer minimal physical contact. Always check policy with adolescents.
  • Cultural filters: Unavoidable biases in what we notice and ignore — must build self-awareness
Telehealth in Psychiatric NursingVideo conferencing, phone/text counseling, mobile apps. Reaches rural, homebound, stigma-averse populations. Key issues: confidentiality, documentation, consent, safety. DOD uses extensively for veterans with PTSD and depression.

🤝 The Therapeutic Nurse-Patient Relationship

Social vs. Therapeutic RelationshipSocial: mutual needs met; roles shift; superficial. Therapeutic: ENTIRELY focused on patient's needs/goals; nurse meets personal needs OUTSIDE the relationship; includes problem-solving and behavior change; evaluated systematically.
Rogers' Three Growth-Fostering Qualities (GEP)
  • Genuineness: congruence; spontaneous not robotic
  • Empathy: UNDERSTAND (not feel) the patient's experience; objectivity preserved
  • Positive Regard: view as worthy; suspend value judgments
⚠️ Boundary Warning Signs
  • Overhelping: doing what patient can do themselves
  • Feeling you are the "only one who understands"
  • Arriving early/staying late to be with one patient
  • Exchanging phone numbers or planning post-discharge contact
Transference & CountertransferenceTransference: patient displaces feelings from past onto nurse.
Countertransference: nurse displaces their own past feelings onto patient.
Both blur boundaries. Clinical supervision is essential.

📅 Phases of the Nurse-Patient Relationship (Peplau) + Clinical Interview

1
Orientation Phase: First meeting. Establish rapport; clarify nurse role; create contract (time/place/duration/goals); discuss confidentiality and its limits; introduce termination date; begin awareness of transference/countertransference.
2
Working Phase: Gather data; promote problem-solving; facilitate behavioral change; overcome resistance; evaluate/redefine goals; promote coping alternatives. Patient may show intense emotions and defense mechanisms.
3
Termination Phase: Summarize achievements; discuss how to apply new skills; address feelings of loss. Begins at orientation and revisited throughout. Patients with abandonment history may show withdrawal or anger.
Clinical Interview SetupPrivate, safe, visible to staff. Same height seating. 45° angle — no desk barrier. Patient NOT between nurse and door. Start broad. Speak briefly. When in doubt, focus on feelings. Note nonverbal cues throughout.

🔭 Pre-Orientation Phase + Goals + Patient Behaviors

Pre-Orientation PhaseBEFORE the first session, the nurse has many thoughts and feelings. This is where self-awareness begins. Nurses should reflect on personal biases, fears, and countertransference potential BEFORE meeting the patient. Often overlooked but clinically important.

4 Goals of the Therapeutic Relationship

  1. Facilitate communication of distressing thoughts and feelings
  2. Assist patients with problem-solving to help facilitate activities of daily living
  3. Help patients examine self-defeating behaviors and test alternatives
  4. Promote self-care and independence

Factors That Promote vs. Impede the Relationship

Positive Factors (Promote Relationship)
  • Consistency — same nurse, predictable approach
  • Pacing — patient sets the pace
  • Active listening — undivided attention
  • Positive initial impressions; comfort; balanced control
Negative Factors (Impede Relationship)
  • Inconsistency — different nurses, unpredictable responses
  • Unavailability — nurse not present when needed
  • Mutual avoidance — both parties withdrawing
  • Lack of self-awareness — unrecognized countertransference
Attending Behaviors — Foundation of InterviewingAttending behaviors must be culturally and individually appropriate. They include: eye contact (direct vs. avoidant — varies by culture), body language (open, relaxed, forward lean = interest), vocal qualities (tone, rate, volume, clarity), and verbal tracking (following the patient's narrative without abrupt topic changes). Positive attending behaviors OPEN communication. Negative attending behaviors INHIBIT expression.
Symmetrical vs. Complementary RelationshipsSymmetrical: Equal relationship between equals — friends, colleagues. Both parties share power equally. Complementary: Difference in status and power — nurse and patient, teacher and student. The relationship is characterized by inequality; one participant has more power/authority. The nurse-patient relationship is a COMPLEMENTARY relationship.

🧬 Module 4: Nursing Process, MSE & Psychopharmacology

How nurses assess, diagnose, and treat — and the neurobiology behind psychotropic medications.

💡

The BIG idea: The nursing process is cyclical. Drug classes work by targeting specific neurotransmitter systems — know the system, predict the effect and side effects.

🔄 The Six-Step Nursing Process

1
Assessment: Comprehensive data — MSE, psychosocial, cultural/spiritual, self-awareness. Primary source = patient. Secondary = family, records, staff.
2
Diagnosis: Problem (unmet need) + Etiology ("related to") + Supporting data ("as evidenced by"). Example: Self-mutilation related to disturbed body image AEB self-cutting.
3
Outcomes Identification: Measurable, patient-centered, time-bound goals.
4
Planning: SAIE — Safe, Appropriate, Individualized, Evidence-Based.
5
Implementation: PMH-RN: coordination, health teaching, milieu therapy, pharmacological monitoring. PMH-APRN: adds psychotherapy and prescriptive authority.
6
Evaluation: Criterion-based, ongoing. Revise diagnoses, outcomes, and interventions as needed. Documentation = the 7th step (legal document).
ACEs — Adverse Childhood ExperiencesPhysical/emotional/sexual abuse, neglect, household violence, substance abuse in household, parental loss. Cause chronic toxic stress → increased risk of mental illness, physical disease, substance abuse, and suicide throughout the lifespan.
Document "Nonadherence" NOT "Noncompliant""Noncompliant" is judgmental and no longer protects nurses legally under ACA. Document: what was taught, patient's understanding, barriers identified, follow-up plan.

📐 Nursing Process — Deeper Details

Types of Nursing Diagnoses

  • Standard nursing diagnosis: Problem + Etiology + Supporting data (signs and symptoms)
  • Risk diagnoses: High probability of a FUTURE negative event for a vulnerable individual (no "as evidenced by" needed — it hasn't happened yet). Example: "Risk for self-directed violence related to hopelessness."
  • Health promotion diagnoses: Willingness to enhance specific health behaviors. Example: "Readiness for enhanced coping."
NIC & NOC — Standardized Nursing LanguageNIC (Nursing Interventions Classification) — provides standardized nursing INTERVENTIONS.
NOC (Nursing Outcomes Classification) — provides standardized nursing OUTCOMES.
Together they create a common language for nursing care across settings and support research and continuity of care.

PMH-RN Implementation Standards (5A–5G)

  • 5A — Coordination of Care: Organize and oversee patient care activities
  • 5B — Health Teaching and Health Promotion: Patient and family education
  • 5E — Pharmacological, Biological, and Integrative Therapies: Medication monitoring, administration, education
  • 5F — Milieu Therapy: Managing therapeutic environment; community groups
  • 5G — Therapeutic Relationship and Counseling: Nurse-patient relationship; basic-level counseling
PMH-APRN ADDITIONAL Roles (Advanced)Prescriptive authority and treatment, psychotherapy (individual/group/family), consultation. These are BEYOND the PMH-RN scope.

Standards of Practice — What They Provide

Why Standards of Practice MatterThe Psychiatric-Mental Health Nursing Standards of Practice provide: (1) criteria for nursing CERTIFICATION, (2) the LEGAL definition of nursing as reflected in many states' nurse practice acts, and (3) the basis for NCLEX-RN content. They define the critical thinking model known as the nursing process.

Documentation — What Must Be Charted (7 Key Items)

Required Documentation Content
  1. Evaluation of stated outcomes
  2. All changes in patient condition
  3. Record of informed consents
  4. Medication reactions (adverse effects)
  5. Symptoms and concerns reported
  6. Untoward incidents (falls, altercations)
  7. Patient progress toward goals
Use "nonadherence" not "noncompliance."
Assessment Documentation Goals
  • Establish rapport
  • Obtain understanding of current problem or chief complaint
  • Review physical status and obtain baseline vital signs
  • Assess for risk factors (suicide/homicide)
  • Perform the MSE
  • Assess psychosocial status
  • Identify mutual goals for treatment
  • Document in a retrievable format

🚨 MAOI Safety — Tyramine & Drug InteractionsMAOIs block the enzyme that breaks down tyramine. ⚠️ Antipsychotic Side Effects — Know These Cold

Extrapyramidal Symptoms (EPS) — FGAs

  • Dystonia: Acute muscle spasm/stiffness (early onset); treat with benztropine (anticholinergic)
  • Akathisia: Restlessness, can't sit still; often mistaken for anxiety
  • Pseudoparkinsonism: Drug-induced Parkinson symptoms — tremor, rigidity, shuffling; treat with benztropine
  • Tardive Dyskinesia (TD): Late-onset; repetitive involuntary movements (lip smacking, tongue thrusting); MAY BE IRREVERSIBLE; monitor with AIMS scale
🚨 NMS — Neuroleptic Malignant SyndromeLIFE-THREATENING. Signs: severe muscle rigidity + fever + altered mental status + autonomic instability. STOP antipsychotic IMMEDIATELY. Dantrolene + supportive care. Can occur with ANY antipsychotic.
SGA (Atypical) Side Effects
  • Metabolic syndrome: Weight gain, elevated glucose, triglycerides, insulin resistance → monitor weight/BMI/fasting glucose/lipids
  • Clozapine: Agranulocytosis (weekly ANC monitoring required); drooling; seizures (dose-related)
  • Risperidone: Hyperprolactinemia → gynecomastia, galactorrhea, sexual dysfunction
  • QTc prolongation: Ziprasidone and others → baseline EKG recommended
Cross-Cultural PsychopharmacologyEthnic variations in drug response due to genetic CYP450 differences AND cultural beliefs about medications. Patient's perception of need, side effect tolerance, and preference for alternatives must all be considered. GeneSight pharmacogenetic testing available.

🧬 Module 4: Pharmacology

MAOI

MAOI Dietary Restriction — "Aged Foods Are Dangerous"

MAOIs block the enzyme that breaks down tyramine. Tyramine-rich foods → tyramine not metabolized → catecholamine flood → HYPERTENSIVE CRISIS.
Foods: aged cheese, cured/fermented meats, red wine, beer, avocados, overripe bananas, soy products.
Restriction continues 2 WEEKS AFTER stopping MAOIs.
Think: "MAOI = Must Avoid Old/Intense foods — even weeks after stopping."
